# The Practitioner's 25

A pinned shortlist for the top of [/explore/mental-models](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models): the 25 models an engineering leader reaches for most, each with a one-line **How I use it** in Naval's own words.

**Status:** approved November 2026 and live on the site. The notes use a warm, professional tone with examples from DevOps, AI and SRE work. The page reads them from `pages/explore/practitioners25.ts`; edit that file to change a note or the order, and update this table to match.


| # | Model | Why it's in the 25 | How I use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | [Arguing from First Principles](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#arguing-from-first-principles) | Cuts through "we've always done it this way" in transformations. | When a team tells me a change approval board is non-negotiable, I gently ask what risk it was created to catch. More often than not, automated tests and a canary release catch that risk better, and much faster. |
| 2 | [Second-Order Thinking](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#second-order-thinking) | Most platform and tooling decisions fail on their second-order effects. | Before we adopt a new observability platform or AI tool, I ask who will be on call for it in two years, and what it will cost them. The licence is rarely the expensive part. |
| 3 | [Inversion](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#inversion) | The fastest way to find risks nobody wants to say out loud. | I start reliability planning by asking, "How could we take production down next month?" The answers become our first set of guardrails. |
| 4 | [Systems Thinking](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#systems-thinking) | DevOps, FinOps and SecOps problems are system problems, not people problems. | When on-call is burning people out, I look at the system first: alert design, release cadence, service ownership. Heroics are usually a symptom, not the fix. |
| 5 | [Feedback Loops](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#feedback-loops) | The core of CI/CD, SRE and FinOps alike. | I judge an engineering practice by how quickly it tells a team they were wrong. A pipeline that fails in five minutes teaches far more than one that fails in an hour. |
| 6 | [Conway's Law](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#conways-law) | Architecture and org design are the same decision. | Before any re-platforming, I sketch the team structure next to the target architecture. If the two don't match, the architecture quietly drifts back to the org chart. |
| 7 | [Theory of Constraints](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#theory-of-constraints) | Stops teams optimising the wrong step. | When delivery feels slow, I look for where work waits longest. Increasingly it's code review rather than coding, especially once AI assistants speed up writing the code. |
| 8 | [Value Stream Mapping](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#value-stream-mapping) | Makes waiting time visible to executives. | My first workshop with a new team maps idea-to-production with the wait time on every step. Seeing weeks of waiting around a couple of days of real work changes the conversation. |
| 9 | [Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule)](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#pareto-principle) | Most cloud cost and most incidents come from a few sources. | I sort incidents and cloud spend by size and start with the top handful. A few noisy alerts or oversized clusters usually account for most of the pain. |
| 10 | [Goodhart's Law](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#goodharts-law) | Protects metric programmes like DORA from gaming. | I always pair a speed metric with a stability one, such as deployment frequency with change failure rate, so improving one can't quietly hurt the other. |
| 11 | [OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#okrs) | Turns transformation goals into measurable outcomes. | I encourage teams to write key results as outcomes, like "time to restore under an hour", rather than tasks like "set up dashboards". The outcome keeps everyone honest. |
| 12 | [Cynefin Framework](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#cynefin-framework) | Tells you whether to plan, analyse or experiment. | It helps me choose how to act: a familiar outage pattern gets a runbook, a new AI use case gets a small safe-to-fail experiment, and a major incident gets stabilised first. |
| 13 | [Reversible vs Irreversible Decisions](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#reversible-vs-irreversible-decisions) | Speeds up most decisions without adding risk. | Teams can decide reversible things on their own, like a feature flag or a config change. We slow down together for the one-way doors: data migrations, vendor lock-in, public APIs. |
| 14 | [OODA Loop](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#ooda-loop) | How good incident response actually works. | During incidents I encourage a quick observe, orient, decide, act rhythm. Rolling back in ten minutes beats a perfect diagnosis in two hours. |
| 15 | [Wardley Mapping](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#wardley-mapping) | Shows what to build, buy or rent. | I map a platform before deciding where our engineers should spend their time. Compute and CI are commodities to buy; the parts that make us different deserve our best people. |
| 16 | [5 Whys](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#5-whys) | The simplest root-cause tool there is. | In post-incident reviews I keep asking "why?" until we reach something the team can actually change, usually a process or a missing guardrail rather than a person. |
| 17 | [Blameless Post-mortem](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#blameless-post-mortem) | Without it, nobody tells you what really happened. | My first question in every review is, "What made this easy to get wrong?" People share far more when they know the goal is learning, not blame. |
| 18 | [Pre-mortem](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#pre-mortem) | Cheapest risk management available. | Before a big migration, the team writes the story of how it failed. Every cause on that list becomes a task, a test or a rollback step. |
| 19 | [Swiss Cheese Model](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#swiss-cheese-model) | Explains why layered controls beat one perfect gate. | When something slips through, I ask which other layer could have caught it (review, tests, canary analysis, alerting), not just how to patch the one that missed. |
| 20 | [Technical Debt](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#technical-debt) | The language that gets engineering work funded. | I describe debt as the interest the business is already paying: slower features, more incidents, longer onboarding. Framed that way, the work gets funded. |
| 21 | [Kaizen](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#kaizen) | Sustains change after the transformation programme ends. | Every retrospective ends with one small improvement and a named owner. Shaving a few minutes off a build each sprint adds up to days over a year. |
| 22 | [Six Thinking Hats](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#six-thinking-hats) | Keeps architecture reviews from becoming debates. | In architecture and AI-adoption reviews, we take one hat at a time so the cautious voices and the creative ones each get a fair hearing. |
| 23 | [Circle of Competence](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#circle-of-competence) | Knowing when to bring in an expert. | I'm open with teams about where my experience ends and theirs begins. The best calls usually come from asking the engineer closest to the problem. |
| 24 | [Eisenhower Matrix](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#eisenhower-matrix) | Protects important, non-urgent work like platform investment. | I protect time each week for important, non-urgent work such as platform improvements and reliability debt, before the urgent requests fill the calendar. |
| 25 | [Margin of Safety](https://nthakur.com/explore/mental-models#margin-of-safety) | Capacity, budgets and timelines all need headroom. | We plan capacity, error budgets and delivery dates with deliberate headroom. Systems, and people, need room to absorb the unexpected. |
